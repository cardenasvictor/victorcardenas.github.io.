**What happened.** Over the past two years, supervisors in Canada, the United States and the euro area have run physical climate risk exercises on bank portfolios. Each stopped at the same place: identifying which exposures sit in high-hazard zones. None published adjustment factors for probability of default or loss given default. The physical side of the ledger still enters credit models, where it enters at all, as one number per exposure — the average annual loss.

**Why it matters.** The average is the right input for the provisioning question: how much expected loss accrues each year across the book. It is the wrong input for the capital question, because catastrophe losses are not spread evenly across years. They concentrate in a few events, in a few assets, in the tail. In work on critical infrastructure in British Columbia, the single-number interface withheld between 28 and 41 percent of 1-in-100-year expected credit loss. Nearly all of that shortfall was unexpected loss, and most of it sat in one name. Rating migration analysis found nothing — not because the risk was absent, but because the metric could not see it.

**What it means for a credit risk officer.** Ask the catastrophe-model provider for more than the mean. Two well-placed tail quantiles per exposure recover most of what the average hides, without requiring the bank to share its credit map or the provider to open its model. The question is not whether to build a climate overlay; it is what the overlay is allowed to carry across the interface.
