**What happened.** The disclosure mandate came first from the TCFD and now from IFRS S2. Both require banks to report climate-related risk. Neither contains a method for quantifying it. Faced with a standard that says what to disclose but not how to compute it, most banks did the reasonable thing: they measured what could be measured. Financed emissions, Scope 1 through 3, PCAF attribution. The numbers are auditable and the regulator accepts them. That is where the majority of the industry stands today.

**Why it matters.** Emissions accounting answers a transition question. It says nothing about what a flood, a hurricane or a wildfire does to probability of default, loss given default, provisions or capital. The institutions that have moved past the inventory stage, and they are a minority, found that the available guidance is a set of proxies rather than measurements. Canada's banking supervisor, OSFI, published adjustment factors for transition risk and stopped short of physical. The European Banking Authority (EBA) permits a climate add-on to risk parameters but leaves its size to judgement. The Basel Committee allows a margin of conservatism for climate data gaps and does not say how large. Each of these is a placeholder for a number nobody has derived.

**What it means for a credit risk officer.** If your institution is still at the emissions stage, that is not a failing. It is the correct response to a standard that did not specify more. If you have moved beyond it, the question in front of you is narrower and harder: how does catastrophic physical risk enter your credit metrics with the information you can actually obtain from a catastrophe model, and no more than that. That question has an answer. My research develops a methodology, grounded in the state of the art, for asking a catastrophe model the right questions so that its output feeds the credit model with exactly the information it needs.
