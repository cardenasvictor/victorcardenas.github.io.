On 14 October, a group of senior professionals from the UK and European insurance and reinsurance industry published an anonymous memo, *Sounding the Alarm*. It is unusually candid. The industry's strategy for a climate that is becoming less predictable, they write, has three steps: reprice cover, restrict it, and where neither works, withdraw it. The 12-month renewal cycle lets this happen "without friction". Catastrophe modellers are being asked to "manage down the numbers" to keep deals flowing. And in the boardrooms, climate issues "barely make the top three" most pressing issues discussed. Their conclusion: uninsured losses will rise, "and someone else will have to pay the price."

The memo is addressed to governments, customers and investors. It does not say who that someone else is. A large part of the answer is the banking system, and banks are not yet looking.

Here is why. A mortgage requires insurance at origination. From that moment, the lender treats the collateral as protected and prices the loan accordingly. But the policy is renewed every year by the borrower and the insurer, in a transaction the bank neither sees nor controls. When the insurer reprices, restricts or withdraws, the bank's collateral loses its protection silently. The insurer is the bank's first line of defence against physical risk, and it is a line of defence the bank has borrowed rather than built.

This is not hypothetical. In the United States, federal flood insurance caps out at USD 250,000 per home, well below the value of much of the coastal housing stock, and insurers have already stopped writing new policies in parts of Florida and California. Lenders do react to new flood-risk information, but modestly and briefly: when New Orleans redrew its flood maps, affected mortgages carried rates about six basis points higher and somewhat lower loan-to-value ratios, and both effects faded within two years (Arseneau and Kara, 2025; see also Blickle, Perry and Santos, 2024). A lender's memory of risk lasts two years; the insurer's decision to stay or leave is taken every twelve months.

The research literature shows the same blind spot from the other side. At the loan level, a mortgage that goes through a major hurricane is 13 to 18 percent more likely to fall 90 days delinquent (Rossi, 2020). At the bank level, the same disasters barely register in reported performance (Blickle, Hamerling and Morgan, 2021). The gap between those two findings is the sum of the buffers: homeowner and flood insurance, federal disaster assistance, the borrower's own equity, loans sold to the government-sponsored enterprises, and, last in line, the bank's own forbearance. Each one absorbs loss before it reaches the balance sheet. Each one can also be withdrawn, and the memo describes precisely how the largest of them will be.

What a bank knows today is its gross exposure. What it does not know is its net loss curve: the probability that a storm's loss, after passing through that chain of buffers, exceeds the capital set aside for it, and how that probability changes when the chain weakens at the next renewal. That is the number the memo's warning turns into a question for every lender with coastal collateral.

Measuring that curve, under protection as it works today and under protection weakened at renewal, is the problem my current research addresses.

The insurers have said what they will do. The open question is whether banks and their supervisors will quantify what that means for them before the renewal that leaves them exposed.
